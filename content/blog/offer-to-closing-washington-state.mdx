# What Happens Between Making an Offer and Closing in Washington?

In Washington State, the journey from an accepted offer to closing day typically takes 30 to 45 days for a financed purchase, though cash deals can close in as few as 7 to 10 days. During that window, your home goes through inspections, appraisal, title review, lender underwriting, and a whole lot of coordination between your agent, the seller's agent, escrow, and your lender.

I know that 30 to 45 day window can feel like a black box if you've never been through it before. So let me walk you through exactly what happens, step by step, in the order it typically unfolds in Pierce County.

## What Is Mutual Acceptance and Why Does It Matter?

Mutual acceptance is the moment both you (the buyer) and the seller have signed the purchase and sale agreement. In Washington, this is the official starting gun for the transaction. Every deadline in the contract counts from this date.

When I say "I got mutual," this is what I mean. It's a big deal. But it's also just the beginning of a process with a lot of moving parts.

Within the first day or two, here's what kicks off:

**Earnest money deposit.** You deliver your earnest money (typically 1% to 3% of the purchase price) to escrow within 2 to 3 business days. This gets applied toward your down payment at closing.

**Escrow opens.** A title and escrow company becomes the neutral third party holding funds and coordinating documents through closing.

**Title search begins.** The title company researches the property's ownership history to confirm the seller has clear title. They'll flag any liens or encumbrances that need resolving.

## What Happens During the Inspection Period?

The inspection period is your due diligence window. In most Pierce County transactions, this runs 10 business days from mutual acceptance, though the timeline is negotiable.

During this period, you'll schedule and complete a home inspection (and I always recommend additional inspections like a sewer scope, septic inspection if applicable, and any specialist inspections the standard inspection flags). After your inspections are complete, you have three options:

**Approve** the condition and proceed. The inspection period closes, and you move forward.

**Request repairs or credits** from the seller using Form 35R. This is a negotiation, and it's one of the most important conversations I have on your behalf during the entire transaction.

**Disapprove** and terminate the agreement. If the inspection reveals something you're not comfortable with, you can walk away with your earnest money refunded.

The inspection period is the most active part of the transaction for buyers. Once it closes, the deal shifts into the lender's hands.

## What Does the Appraisal Process Look Like?

If you're financing your purchase (which most buyers in Pierce County are), your lender will order an appraisal after the inspection period clears. The appraiser is an independent, licensed professional who visits the property and determines its market value.

The appraisal serves the lender's interest: they want to make sure the home is worth at least as much as the amount they're lending you. If the appraisal comes back at or above the purchase price, you're good. If it comes back low, that's a conversation.

A low appraisal gives you a few options:

**Negotiate a price reduction** with the seller to match the appraised value.

**Make up the difference** by bringing additional cash to closing (the gap between the appraised value and the purchase price).

**Walk away** if your contract includes an appraisal contingency (and it should).

**Challenge the appraisal** through a reconsideration of value if your agent can provide comparable sales the appraiser missed.

Appraisals in Pierce County typically take 1 to 2 weeks from the order date. The fee runs $500 to $800, paid by the buyer upfront.

## What Is the Lender Doing During All of This?

While you're handling inspections and the appraiser is doing their work, your lender is processing your loan. This is called underwriting, and it's where the lender verifies everything: your income, employment, assets, credit, debts, and the property itself.

Here's my biggest piece of advice for this phase: don't make any major financial changes. No new credit cards, no large purchases, no job changes, no unexplained deposits. Your lender is watching your financial picture closely, and changes during underwriting can delay or kill your closing.

I've seen a buyer almost lose a deal because they bought furniture on a credit card before closing. The new debt nearly pushed their debt-to-income ratio over the limit. Don't be that buyer. When your lender asks for documents, respond quickly. The faster you turn things around, the smoother this phase goes.

## What Happens With Title, Escrow, and the Closing Disclosure?

While all of this is happening, the title and escrow company is working behind the scenes. The title company produces a preliminary title commitment showing any easements, CC&Rs, liens, or encumbrances on the property. I review this with my buyers because sometimes there are surprises that affect how you'll use the property.

The escrow officer is essentially the project manager of the closing, coordinating between your lender, both agents, and both parties to make sure all the money and documents land in the right place at the right time.

About a week before closing, your lender issues the Closing Disclosure (CD), a line-by-line breakdown of every cost in the transaction. Federal law requires you to receive this at least **3 business days before closing**. I compare it against your original loan estimate to flag anything that shifted. If something doesn't look right, this is the time to ask.

## What Do Signing Day and Key Handoff Look Like?

In Washington, buyers and sellers don't have to sign at the same time. You'll visit the escrow company 1 to 3 days before closing to sign loan documents and closing paperwork. Plan for about an hour, and ask questions about anything you don't understand.

After you sign, the documents go back to your lender for funding. Once the lender sends the money to escrow, the escrow company records the deed with the Pierce County Auditor's office. Once it's recorded, the property is legally yours. Recording typically happens the same day as funding or the next business day.

Honestly, the key handoff is my favorite part of every transaction. All the negotiating, the inspection stress, the document signing, it all builds to this moment where someone walks into a home that's theirs.

## How Long Does the Whole Process Take?

Here's the typical timeline for a financed purchase in Pierce County:

- **Days 1 to 3:** Deliver earnest money, open escrow, schedule inspections
- **Days 1 to 10:** Complete inspections, negotiate repairs or credits
- **Days 10 to 25:** Appraisal, lender underwriting, title review
- **Days 25 to 35:** Final loan approval, closing disclosure issued
- **Days 35 to 40:** Sign documents, lender funds, deed records
- **Closing day:** Keys in hand

Cash deals compress this significantly. I've closed cash purchases in Pierce County in as few as 10 days.
