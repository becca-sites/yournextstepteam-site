# Local Vendors I Trust: Inspectors, Lenders, and Contractors in Pierce County

Here's something nobody tells you when you're buying or selling a home: your agent is only one person on a much bigger team. Behind every smooth closing, there's a home inspector, a lender, a title company, maybe a contractor or two, and sometimes a septic inspector for good measure.

I've closed over 270 deals in Pierce County. That's a lot of opportunities to learn which vendors show up and which ones don't. I keep a vetted list. I'm protective of it. And I'm not naming names in a blog post (people change companies, retire, move), but I will tell you exactly what "good" looks like in each category.

## What Makes a Good Home Inspector?

**Licensing.** In Washington state, home inspectors must be licensed under RCW 18.280. Ask for their license number and verify it through the Department of Licensing. No license? Walk away.

**Insurance.** Errors and omissions (E&O) insurance protects you if the inspector misses something significant. Ask if they carry it.

**A sample report.** Before you hire anyone, ask to see one. You want detailed notes, clear organization, photos, and plain-language explanations. If the sample is three pages of vague checkboxes, that tells you something.

**Communication style.** A good inspector walks you through the property after, explains findings, and helps you understand the difference between "this is cosmetic" and "this needs attention now."

**Red flags:** Inspectors who rush (a thorough inspection takes 2 to 3 hours for an average home), won't let you attend, or seem reluctant to put things in writing.

A standard inspection in Pierce County typically runs $400 to $700 depending on size and age. Specialty inspections (sewer scope, radon, mold) are additional.

## Should You Use a Local Lender or a Big Bank?

My answer is pretty clear: I prefer working with local lenders. Not because big banks can't get the job done, but because local lenders pick up the phone.

That matters more than you think. Real estate transactions move fast. When your loan officer is three time zones away and you can't reach a human, small issues become big delays.

Here's what to ask any lender:

- **Pre-approval vs. pre-qualification?** Pre-qualification is a rough estimate. Pre-approval means they've actually reviewed your financials. You want a pre-approval before making offers.
- **What are your rates and fees?** Get this in writing. Compare Loan Estimate documents, not just interest rates.
- **What's your average closing timeline?** A lender who can close in 21 days has an advantage over one who needs 45.
- **Will you be my contact throughout, or will I get handed off?**
- **Are you licensed in Washington state?** Verify through the NMLS Consumer Access database.

Also, get pre-approved before you start looking at houses. Not during. Before.

## What Should You Look for in a Contractor?

Whether it's pre-listing repairs or post-inspection fixes, here's my advice:

**Bundle small jobs.** Pro tip: a handyman needs to make the trip worth their while. A single doorknob replacement costs a premium because travel and setup eat into their day. Batch five or six small jobs into one visit, and the per-item cost drops significantly. Make a punch list, not a phone-call-per-item list.

**Check licensing.** Washington state contractors need to be registered with the Department of Labor & Industries (L&I) for work above a certain threshold. Look up their registration, bond, and insurance status on the L&I website.

**Get multiple bids for bigger projects.** Three is standard. If one comes in dramatically lower, ask why.

**Get everything in writing.** Scope, timeline, payment schedule, warranty. A handshake is fine for moving a couch. It's not fine for a $15,000 kitchen update.

## What Does a Title Company Actually Do?

Title companies handle a lot of behind-the-scenes work. The short version: they make sure the property you're buying actually belongs to the person selling it, and that there aren't surprises attached.

Specifically, they conduct a title search for liens and encumbrances, issue title insurance, handle the escrow process, and coordinate closing. A good title company communicates proactively and flags issues early. I have preferences because I've seen the difference between that and the company that goes silent for two weeks and then asks for documents at the last minute.

## What Should Buyers Know About Appraisals?

If you're getting a mortgage, your lender orders an appraisal. Quick facts:

- **You don't choose the appraiser.** The lender orders through an appraisal management company to maintain independence.
- **Low appraisals happen.** If the property appraises below the purchase price, you can renegotiate, bring extra cash, or request a reconsideration of value.
- **The fee runs $500 to $800** in Pierce County and is paid upfront.

I always prep my listings for appraisals by providing comparable sales and improvement details. It doesn't guarantee anything, but it gives the appraiser the full picture.

## Why Does Pierce County Need Septic Inspectors?

If you're buying in Graham, Spanaway, Eatonville, or the more rural parts of Pierce County, there's a solid chance the property is on septic rather than municipal sewer.

**Dye tests** are the most common method. The inspector introduces a non-toxic dye and checks for surface breakout. Pierce County typically requires one for property transfers.

**Pumping.** Tanks should be pumped every 3 to 5 years. If the seller can't provide records, that's worth noting. I always recommend a pump and inspection as part of the sale.

**Cost:** A septic inspection with dye test runs $200 to $500. A full pump-out is $300 to $600 depending on tank size.

**The bigger picture.** A failing septic system costs $15,000 to $30,000+ to replace. This is not an inspection you skip.
