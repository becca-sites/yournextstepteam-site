## Zero days on market

That's what the record says, and it tells you exactly what position my buyers were in. This house didn't sit. It didn't get a price cut. It didn't wait around for anybody to think it over.

The people who got it were two first-time buyers in their early twenties, referred to me by past clients. They wanted this house badly.

Then, in the inspection window, we found a patch of the backyard that was noticeably wetter than the rest of the lot. And the property is on septic.

## What that wet patch actually meant

Here's the honest version, because the way most agents would tell this story is not quite true.

Pierce County had already passed the system through RSS approval. On paper it was fine. But a septic replacement out here can run $30,000, and for two people in their early twenties who had just put everything they had into a down payment, that number was the difference between a good decision and a disaster.

So were they going to walk? No. They were buying this house either way, and I knew it.

What they were going to do, if the system was compromised, was ask the seller for a significant contribution toward the repair. If the seller said no, they'd have closed anyway. We just weren't going to tell the seller that.

Which means the real question was never "do we buy this house." It was "do we have a legitimate ask, and can we prove it."

That distinction matters, and almost nobody writes about it. An inspection finding is only worth something at the negotiating table if you can demonstrate it. A vague bad feeling about a soggy lawn gets you nothing. Evidence gets you a credit.

## So I went and got the dye

The UV dye test was my idea.

I didn't order it. I didn't refer it out. I didn't wait three days for a contractor to have an opening on a deal that was already under contract on a zero-day listing. I went to the supply store, bought the dye, called the seller for permission, and poured it into the septic tank.

Here's how it works. The dye travels through the system. If there's a leak, or the drain field is surfacing where it shouldn't, the dye comes up with the water. Under a UV light in the dark, it glows.

My buyers went out that night with a UV flashlight, looking for grass that lit up. I came back the next morning and walked the yard in daylight to check for anything that had surfaced overnight.

Neither pass turned up a thing.

## It was just the lay of the land

The septic system was fine. That soggy corner was natural water, most likely an underground spring or runoff coming down off the hill. Just the natural lay of the land.

So there was no repair ask, because there was nothing to ask about. They closed that spring at full price, around $625,000, on a house that had been available for zero days.

And they were thrilled, because they wanted this house pretty badly and they didn't want a $30,000 repair bill hanging over them for the next couple of years.

## What I did that another agent wouldn't have

I'll be specific, because this is the whole point.

Another agent would have forwarded the county's RSS approval and called it handled. That's technically defensible. Nobody would have called it wrong.

What I did instead:

1. **I treated the question as a negotiating question, not an emotional one.** I knew my buyers weren't walking. So I didn't spend their inspection window managing their feelings. I spent it building or killing a case for a repair credit.
2. **I did the test myself, same day.** Buying the dye and pouring it in the tank is not in anybody's job description. On a listing that went pending immediately, waiting for a vendor would have burned the window.
3. **I asked the seller's permission first.** You do not put anything into somebody else's septic system without a yes. That's the part that keeps a creative move from becoming a liability.
4. **I checked it twice, at two different times of day.** The buyers looked under UV at night. I looked in daylight the next morning. Two passes, two sets of eyes, one answer.

Clean answer, fast, no cost to my clients, and they signed knowing instead of hoping.

## What I'd tell anyone buying on septic in Pierce County

A lot of homes out toward Waller Road, and through Spanaway, Graham, and the rural pockets east of Puyallup, are on septic rather than city sewer. If you're shopping in that part of the county, you'll run into it.

**A county-passed inspection is real, but it's a snapshot.** It tells you the system performed on the day it was tested. It doesn't answer every question you might reasonably have.

**Wet ground is not automatically a failing system.** Grade, drainage, a downspout dumping in the wrong spot, a seasonal high water table, an underground spring. All of these make a soggy yard and none of them are a septic problem.

**But you're allowed to find out.** A dye test isn't expensive and it isn't exotic. If something is bothering you during your inspection window, that's exactly what the window is for.

**And know what you'd do with the answer before you go looking for it.** If the test comes back bad, what's your ask? If the seller says no, what then? Decide that first. Otherwise you're just collecting anxiety.

## Questions I get about buying a home on septic in Pierce County

**Does a soggy backyard mean the septic is failing?**
Often not. Grade, drainage, downspouts, springs, and a seasonal high water table all produce wet ground. A failing drain field is one possible cause among several, and it's testable.

**What is a septic dye test?**
Dye goes into the system and you track whether it surfaces where it shouldn't. UV dye is read under ultraviolet light after dark, which is why these sometimes happen at night.

**If the county already inspected it, do I need my own test?**
Not always. But if you have a specific concern the county inspection doesn't address, you're entitled to investigate during your inspection window.

**What does a septic system cost to replace in Pierce County?**
Enough that it's worth a few dollars of dye to find out you don't need one. Real costs vary a lot by system type, soil, and site.

**Can first-time buyers compete for a house that goes pending immediately?**
Yes. This one did, at around $625,000, with zero days on market. Being ready and being able to move fast matters more than people expect.
