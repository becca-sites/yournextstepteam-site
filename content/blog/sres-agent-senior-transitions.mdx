# What Is an SRES Agent and Why Does It Matter for Senior Transitions?

An SRES (Seniors Real Estate Specialist) is a real estate agent who has completed specialized training through the National Association of Realtors to serve clients aged 50 and older through the unique financial, emotional, and logistical challenges of buying or selling a home later in life. It matters because selling a home you've lived in for 20 or 30 years is nothing like selling a starter home, and the agent who helps you through it needs to understand the differences.

I hold the SRES designation because senior transitions are a core part of the work I do in Pierce County. A significant number of my clients are older adults who are downsizing, relocating to be closer to family, or transitioning into assisted living or adult family homes. These are not simple transactions. They're life transitions that happen to involve real estate, and they require a different kind of patience, knowledge, and care.

## What Training Does an SRES Agent Have?

The SRES designation isn't just a line on a business card. It's a curriculum developed by the National Association of Realtors that covers the specific issues older adults face when making real estate decisions.

Here's what the training covers:

**Housing options for aging adults.** Not every senior is moving into a nursing home. The SRES curriculum covers the full spectrum: independent living, 55+ communities, adult family homes, assisted living, continuing care retirement communities, and aging in place. I help my clients understand all their options, not just the one they've been told about.

**Financial considerations unique to seniors.** This includes using retirement accounts (pensions, 401(k)s, IRAs) in real estate transactions, tax implications of selling a long-held primary residence (including the capital gains exclusion), and how reverse mortgages work. The financial picture for someone who's owned their home for 25 years is very different from a first-time seller.

**Medicare, Medicaid, and Social Security impacts.** The proceeds from a home sale can affect Medicaid eligibility, and the timing matters. An SRES agent understands these intersections and knows when to bring in a financial advisor or elder law attorney.

**Fraud and financial exploitation awareness.** Older adults are disproportionately targeted by real estate and mortgage fraud. The SRES training teaches agents to recognize warning signs. I take this seriously. Protecting my clients is not negotiable.

## Why Does the Senior Real Estate Experience Require a Specialist?

I think the simplest way to explain this is to describe what a typical senior transaction looks like compared to a typical first-time buyer transaction.

A first-time buyer is usually excited, forward-looking, and working with a relatively clean financial picture. They're buying their first home. It's a milestone.

A senior selling a long-held home is often navigating multiple layers of complexity at the same time. They may be:

**Processing a major life change.** The decision to sell often comes after a health event, the loss of a spouse, or a gradual realization that maintaining the home has become too much. This isn't a "let's upgrade" conversation. It's a "my life is changing and I need help figuring out what comes next" conversation.

**Dealing with decades of belongings.** Sorting, donating, and letting go of 30 years' worth of possessions is emotionally and physically exhausting. I connect my clients with estate sale professionals, donation services, and move managers who specialize in senior relocations so they're not doing this alone.

**Managing family dynamics.** Adult children often have opinions (sometimes conflicting ones) about what Mom or Dad should do. I've sat in conversations where three siblings had three different ideas about whether to sell and where the parent should move. Keeping the client's wishes at the center while respecting everyone involved is part of the work.

**Coordinating the sale with the next step.** Where is the client going next? Do they need to secure a spot in an adult family home before closing? Do they need the proceeds to fund the next living arrangement? The timing of the sale and the move has to line up.

**Working with power of attorney.** Sometimes a family member with power of attorney is managing the sale on behalf of a parent. This adds legal complexity the agent needs to be equipped to handle.

None of these dynamics show up in a typical real estate transaction. They all show up in senior transitions, sometimes all at once.

## What Does This Look Like in Practice in Pierce County?

Pierce County has a growing population of older adults, and many of them have owned their homes for decades. The homes I list for senior clients tend to be well-maintained but may need updating to appeal to today's buyers. The sellers often have significant equity but haven't been tracking the market closely.

Here's what my process looks like when I work with a senior seller in Pierce County:

**Initial conversation (not a sales pitch).** I sit down with the client and often their family to understand the full picture: why they're considering a sale, the timeline, where they want to go next, and what concerns they have. This conversation usually takes an hour or more, and it's one of the most important hours I spend on any listing.

**Home assessment with sensitivity.** When I walk through a long-held home, I'm looking at what needs to happen for the market and how to communicate that respectfully. Telling a client their kitchen needs updating is different when they remodeled it themselves in 1998 and it represents one of their proudest memories.

**Connecting with transition resources.** I maintain a network of move managers, estate sale companies, elder law attorneys, and care placement specialists. I don't just hand my clients a list of names. I make introductions and stay involved.

**Flexible timeline.** Senior transitions don't run on the same clock as other sales. Sometimes the client needs time to process the emotional side before they're ready to list. I don't push a timeline that doesn't serve the client.

## How Do I Know If I Need an SRES Agent?

You might benefit from working with an SRES agent if:

You or your parent has lived in the home for 15 or more years, and the sale involves significant equity, emotional attachment, or estate planning considerations.

The sale is connected to a health event, caregiving decision, or transition into a different living arrangement.

There are family members involved in the decision-making process who may need coordination and communication.

The homeowner is dealing with a large volume of personal belongings that need to be sorted before the home can be prepared for sale.

Financial considerations include retirement accounts, Social Security, Medicaid eligibility, or estate planning.

Not every agent over 50 is an SRES, and not every SRES is the right fit. Look for an agent who combines the designation with actual experience doing this work, someone who has sat across the table from a family in transition and knows how to guide them through it with patience and care.
